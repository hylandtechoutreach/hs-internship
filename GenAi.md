# Generative A.I. Guidelines
While it may be important for you to build awareness and comfort with A.I. tools, the ultimate goal of this internship program is to provide an educational and developmental experience. Success for this program is not measured by efficiency or productivity, but by how much you learn and understand. A.I. may be part of that, but it must sit atop a fundamental and foundational knowledge of software development as a whole. These guidelines reflect our mission to provide meaningful and impactful educational experiences, and our focus on building knowledge over products.

## Approved Tools
Hyland has approved [several A.I. tools](https://hyland.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/GA/pages/928055311/Approved+Use+Cases) for productivity enhancement. The two most relevant tools are:

- [Microsoft Copilot](https://copilot.microsoft.com/) (read the [Use Case Guidelines](https://hyland.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/GA/pages/929927115/Microsoft+Copilot+Formerly+Bing+Chat) first)
- [GitHub Copilot](https://github.com/features/copilot) (read the [Use Case Guidelines](https://hyland.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/GA/pages/929765271/Github+Copilot+for+Business) first)

Be sure to adhere to all guidelines for each specific tool. You are **not** required to use these tools.

## Essential Guidelines
If you do decide to use generative A.I., you should:

- Understand each code output at the same level as if you wrote it yourself
- Ask for sources and explanations to ensure you fully understand everything
- Look out for low-quality code or security vulnerabilities
- Perform code reviews on A.I.-generated code, and have at least one additional peer review it as well

Also, you should always consider everything and how everything is affecting everything.

## The Future of Software Development
It is difficult to predict what A.I. will do in the next decade, year, or even in the next month. Software development itself is at the very crucible of this technology: on one hand, software developers (especially entry-level developers) are at high risk of being _replaced_ by A.I.; on the other hand, software developers are the ones who are actually _developing_ A.I. tools. Some questions to consider:

- What's the point of learning to code when A.I. is so good at coding?
- If A.I. replaces junior developers, what happens when senior developers retire?
- How can you build an A.I. agent if you don't know what an API is?
- Does the world need more software engineers? ([The World Needs More Software Engineers](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/the-world-needs-more-software-engineers/))
- Is prompt engineering the same as programming?
- Is high-level programming (e.g., Python) the same as low-level programming (e.g., Assembly)?
- Is coding fun?

## The Future In General
Beyond the world of software, A.I. has the potential to change a lot of things in potentially incomprehensible ways. Right now, it seems that most companies are trying to capitalize on the current moment and momentum to leverage A.I. to meet some sort of business goals or revolutionize their productivity or something like that. That may be a good move in 2026, but it is critical to think far beyond this quarter, this year, or even this decade. Someone has to play the long game here. Someone has to figure out what happens when you see this technology through to its logical conclusion. Some might guess that a [technological singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_singularity) will take place within our lifetime. Or maybe we will [all die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_risk_from_artificial_intelligence). A lot of this could depend on [A.I. alignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_alignment). Or maybe A.I. has already taken over and we are trapped within a [simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation_hypothesis) of its own design.

Anyway, it's important to think about this stuff when you use ChatGPT or whatever :-)
